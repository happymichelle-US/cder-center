About CDER

Mission, history, and the people behind the centre

The Center for Parallel and Distributed Computing Curriculum Development and Educational Resources has worked for more than a decade to bring PDC concepts into the early and core undergraduate computing curriculum.

Mission & vision

Modernising the model of computation students learn first

Modern software is parallel, distributed, networked, event-driven, graphical, API-based and data-intensive. Introductory computing still commonly begins with a sequential-first model that predates the distribution of computing across the Internet and the ubiquity of multicore processors.

CDER exists to close that gap: by developing flexible core curricula and comprehensive instructional materials, facilitating global access to state-of-the-art hardware and software, and organising workshops and competitions that build a durable professional community of PDC educators.

The goal is not to turn first-year students into parallel programmers. It is to give them an early conceptual model in which computation may involve multiple workers, remote data, performance trade-offs, and coordination among interacting components — while preserving the basic learning outcomes of the course.

Curriculum

Define what every CS/CE undergraduate should know about PDC, with Bloom levels and course mappings — and keep it current.

Materials

Publish free, classroom-tested courseware, book chapters, assignments and assessment instruments that instructors can adopt directly.

Community

Train, fund and connect instructors through Early Adopter awards, the Edu* workshops, SIGCSE sessions and summer CyberTraining institutes.

Evidence

Instrument interventions with pre/post survey instruments so adoption decisions rest on data, not enthusiasm.

History

From a curriculum question to national course models

  1. 2010

    The question

    The IEEE Computer Society Technical Committee on Parallel Processing (TCPP) Curriculum Initiative begins with one question: what should every CS and CE undergraduate know about parallel and distributed computing? A preliminary version is released in December.

  2. 2012

    Version 1 of the NSF/TCPP curriculum

    Core PDC topics across architecture, programming, algorithms and cross-cutting areas, each with an expected Bloom level. Over 100 early-adopter institutions worldwide; the guideline informs the ACM/IEEE Computer Science Curricula 2013 report.

  3. 2015 · 2018

    The CDER book series

    Volume 1 (Introducing Concurrency in Undergraduate Courses, Morgan Kaufmann) and Volume 2 (Enhancing the Undergraduate Curriculum, Springer) collect instructor- and student-facing chapters. Free preprints on the CDER site pass tens of thousands of downloads.

  4. 2010 – present

    Early Adopters and the Edu* workshops

    More than 140 early adopters funded by NSF, TCPP, Intel, NVIDIA and IBM. EduPar (with IPDPS), EduHPC (with SC) and EduHiPC (with HiPC) turn isolated adoption into an ongoing professional community of roughly 300 participants and institutions.

  5. 2020

    Curriculum Version 2.0-beta

    A major revision expanding coverage of Big Data, energy and distributed computing, reorganised into four topic tables plus emerging topics. CS2023 includes a dedicated PDC knowledge area citing this effort.

  6. 2023 – 2027

    The CS1/CS2 Exemplar Project

    NSF CyberTraining Award #2321015. Two development teams, six testing institutions, one shared evaluation design — producing complete, classroom-tested introductory course packages rather than isolated modules.

  7. Late 2026

    Volume 3 and what comes next

    A third CDER volume focused on adopter experience, exemplars and resources is expected from Springer. A new NSF award will extend instructor training to infuse AI and Big Data — together with PDC — into CS1, CS2 and Computer Systems courses.

What CDER is building

Flagship

CS1/CS2 Exemplar Project

NSF Award #2321015 · 2023–2027

CyberTraining: Implementation: Medium — Modern Course Exemplars infused with Parallel and Distributed Computing for the Introductory Computing Course Sequence. Employers report that graduates are under-prepared for modern systems; academics report that the blocker is the absence of modernised course sequences to copy. This project goes first: it designs, implements, transfers and rigorously evaluates a PDC-infused first-year sequence across eight diverse institutions.

Development teams

Tennessee Tech (C++) and Knox College (Java) built the original CS1 and CS2 exemplars in two deliberately different institutional contexts.

Testing teams

Casper College, Hawaii Pacific, Montclair State, U. Southern Indiana, U. Nebraska–Lincoln and Webster adopted, adapted and evaluated them.

Backbone team

Prasad, Sussman, Thota, Vaidyanathan and Weems coordinated the project, training workshops and evaluation design.

The guidelines

NSF/IEEE-TCPP Curriculum Initiative

Core PDC topics for undergraduates — organised into architecture, programming and algorithms, plus pervasive concepts that cut across all three.

Each topic carries an expected Bloom level of coverage and learning outcomes for core courses, with additional levels and outcomes where a topic also belongs in an advanced or elective course, plus suggestions for which courses should host it.

Version 2.0-beta is a major revision of the 2012 guideline. It expands coverage of Big Data, energy and distributed computing, integrates those aspects into the three area tables, adds a fourth table for emerging topics, and reorganises the whole set.

How to cite: Prasad, S. K., Estrada, T., Ghafoor, S., Gupta, A., Kant, K., Stunkel, C., Sussman, A., Vaidyanathan, R., Weems, C., et al. 2020. NSF/IEEE-TCPP Curriculum Initiative on Parallel and Distributed Computing — Core Topics for Undergraduates, Version II-beta. 53 pages.

Courses

Where PDC fits in a degree

CDER material is organised around the courses that actually exist in most departments, rather than around a hypothetical PDC elective.

CS1

Introductory programming

Unplugged activities, animations, and light programming that attach PDC ideas to loops, arrays, functions and event handling. Eight complete institutional exemplars.

CS2

Data structures & programming II

Fork–join parallelism, parallel sorting and searching, multithreaded image processing, connection pooling, event-driven game frameworks.

DSA

Data structures & algorithms

OpenMP work-sharing constructs alongside conventional algorithm analysis; work, span and Amdahl's law as first-class analytical tools.

Systems

Computer systems & organisation

Multicore architecture, memory hierarchy and energy — the architecture half of the curriculum guideline.

Electives

PDC & HPC electives

Advanced Bloom levels for every core topic, plus emerging areas: accelerators, heterogeneous systems and cloud.

Non-majors

Codeless modules

Visualisations, simulations and demonstrations that require no programming — suitable for computing and non-computing majors alike.

Early Adopters Program

Seed funding, equipment, and a cohort

Curriculum guidelines alone do not change courses. The Early Adopter competitions exist to give instructors the incentive, the community and the evidence base to actually try something.

Awards funded by NSF, TCPP, Intel, NVIDIA and IBM have provided modest seed funding and equipment to more than 140 early adopters across multiple competitions. Their broader purpose was to create a community of practitioners who test, adapt and refine PDC curricular ideas in varied institutional settings — and report back.

What you get

Seed funding, access to equipment and the CDER cluster, mentoring, and a venue at Edu* to publish your experience report.

What you give

An honest account of what worked, what didn't, and the evaluation data — which becomes material other adopters rely on.

FAQ

Questions instructors actually ask

Isn't PDC too advanced for a first-year course?

This is the most common objection, and the project's answer is empirical: unplugged activities that model processors, data partitioning, speedup and load imbalance work well with students who have written almost no code. The goal at this level is conceptual — that computation may involve multiple workers, remote data and coordination — not implementation skill.

There's no room in my syllabus. Where would this even go?

Analysis of widely used textbooks and syllabi found substantial time spent on artefacts of a 1980s computing model — console text parsing, manual output formatting, hand-built linked structures from primitives. Modernising those units frees the space. Several exemplars also overlay PDC on existing topics: a lesson on loops gains OpenMP pragmas; an already-taught recursive mergesort gains a fork–join version.

Do I need a cluster, a GPU, or special software?

No. Several exemplars require nothing beyond paper, markers and the compiler you already use. Where hardware helps, the CDER Cluster provides free access. Casper College's physical computing modules use inexpensive microcontrollers; UNL's codeless modules run entirely in a browser.

Which programming language do I need?

Exemplars exist in Java, C++, C and Python. The comparison table in the exemplar e-book lets you filter by language first — but the more useful filter is usually activity scale, preparation time and class size.

How much preparation time does a first adoption take?

The recommended strategy is to choose the simplest version of one activity that fits your constraints — often a single 30–50 minute class session with printed handouts. After one offering you can expand it, add a plugged-in component, or collect more systematic evidence.

Is any of this peer reviewed?

Yes. Peachy Assignments are peer reviewed at EduPar and EduHPC; the book volumes are published by Morgan Kaufmann and Springer; JPDC special issues carry the research results; and institutional exemplars report IRB-approved pre/post evaluation data.

Can I contribute my own material?

Yes — register for a free account to upload courseware, or submit a Peachy Assignment for peer review. Experience reports from adopters are welcome at any Edu* workshop.

Can someone help me choose and adapt materials?

Limited consultation from project personnel is available to help adopters select and adapt materials. Get in touch via the Contact page.
